Water Damage Categories 1, 2 and 3 Explained for Waunakee, WI

The three categories in the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, and what each means for your house.

By the Waunakee Water Damage Pros teamUpdated November 2026

Restoration professionals classify water by how contaminated it is, using the three categories in the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard. The category decides the safety steps, what can be dried and what must be discarded, and it is one of the first things a crew asks about.

This guide explains each category with examples, why water can move up a category over time and how the category affects your insurance conversation. It is general information, and a crew classifies the water at the site.

If you are not sure what kind of water you have, treat it as contaminated until someone checks.

The three categories at a glance

This is the core of the standard.

CategoryTypical sourceWhat it means for the job
Category 1Supply line, faucet, tank overflowClean at the source. Most materials can be dried in place if started quickly.
Category 2Washer or dishwasher discharge, sump overflowContaminated enough to cause illness. Porous materials such as carpet pad usually come out.
Category 3Sewage, rising floodwaterGrossly contaminated. Porous materials are removed and surfaces cleaned and sanitized under safety controls.

Category 1: clean water

Clean water comes from a sanitary source, such as a broken supply line or a leaking faucet. It poses no immediate health risk, and most materials can be dried if the work starts quickly. See the burst pipe page for an example.

Clean does not mean harmless forever. Time, temperature and contact with building materials can degrade it, so a clean leak left for a day or two can no longer be treated as clean.

Category 2: gray water

Gray water has some contamination and can cause illness if people are exposed. Typical sources are washing machine and dishwasher discharge, toilet overflow with urine only and sump pump overflow. Carpet pad and other porous materials usually come out, and hard surfaces are cleaned.

Many basement losses start as Category 2 because groundwater and sump overflow carry soil and bacteria. See basement cleanup.

Category 3: black water

Black water is grossly contaminated. It includes sewage, floodwater from rivers and streams, and standing water that has supported growth. Porous materials are removed and discarded, and hard surfaces are cleaned and sanitized while the crew wears protective gear. See sewage backup cleanup and flood damage cleanup.

Do not clean Category 3 water yourself. Keep people and pets away and call.

Why categories matter for coverage in Wisconsin

The Wisconsin Office of the Commissioner of Insurance says sewer backup and sump pump overflow generally need a special endorsement, and flood generally needs a separate policy. Those events are Category 2 or 3, which means the category often points to the coverage question before the cleanup begins.

In Waunakee, Dane County's 2017 hazard mitigation plan lists one structure in the 100-year floodplain and 22 in the 500-year floodplain. Regional storms like the August 2018 event turn many basement losses into Category 2 or 3. The same applies in Dane, DeForest and Mazomanie.

Signs water has moved up a category

  • It has sat for more than a day or two.
  • It passed through insulation, carpet or soil.
  • It has a noticeable odor or color.
  • It came from outside the home or from a drain.
  • There is visible growth on wet materials.

What changes on a Category 2 or 3 job

On higher-category jobs the crew sets up containment, wears protective equipment and removes porous materials before drying begins. Air scrubbers clean the air during demolition, and surfaces are cleaned and treated with antimicrobial products. These steps add time and cost, and they protect everyone who enters the space.

For the household, the practical rules are to keep people and pets out of the area, avoid handling affected materials and leave the removal to the crew.

Who should stay out of a contaminated area

  • Children and infants.
  • Pets.
  • People who are pregnant or immunocompromised.
  • Anyone with open cuts or wounds.
  • Anyone without protective gear.

Why the standard exists

The categories come from the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, a consensus document written by the restoration industry. It gives crews a shared language so that a job in Waunakee is classified the same way as a job anywhere else. Insurers and adjusters read the same categories, which is why a crew's record helps you.

The standard also defines classes of water damage by how much area and material is wet, which determine how much equipment is needed. Ask your crew which class and category apply to your loss.

When in doubt, ask

If you cannot tell the category, say what you know: where the water came from, how long ago, what it touched and what it smells like. A crew can usually narrow the category from those facts before arriving, and confirms it on site with a closer look.

Water category questions

Can clean water become Category 2?

Yes. Time and contact with building materials can degrade it, which is why quick action matters.

Who decides the water category?

A restoration professional classifies it on site, using the S500 standard.

Does a Waunakee basement flood usually count as Category 2 or 3?

Often yes, because groundwater, sump overflow and storm runoff carry contaminants.

Does the category affect insurance?

It often points to the coverage question, since sewer backup, sump overflow and flood generally need special coverage.
